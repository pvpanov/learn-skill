You have three years of daily sales and want to know how well a forecasting model will do next quarter. Whether you split the rows for testing at random or by date decides whether the accuracy you report says anything about that question.

Here is a concrete case. The data run from January 2023 to December 2025, about 1,100 days, and sales drift upward by roughly ten percent a year. A random 80/20 split puts about 220 test days scattered through all three years, so almost every test day has training days on both sides of it, usually the day before and the day after.

A model that partly memorises the local level of the series does very well on such a test day, because it can read the level off the neighbours it was trained on. That is interpolation. Next quarter has no neighbours after it: the model has to carry the trend past the last day it saw, which is extrapolation, so the random-split score measures an easier task than the one you care about. In the example, a model that simply averages the two neighbouring days would score excellently on the random split and could not forecast at all, because the neighbour on the far side of tomorrow does not exist yet.

A time-ordered split puts every test row after every training row: train on January 2023 to June 2025 and test on the last six months. Each test prediction is then made from the past only, which is exactly the situation of the real forecast. The score is usually worse than the random-split score, and the gap is information in itself: it shows how much of the apparent accuracy came from seeing the future's neighbours rather than from skill. The takeaway for your forecast: report the time-ordered score, and if you tune hyperparameters, tune them on a validation window that itself sits before the test window, so the test months are never seen during any choice you make.
